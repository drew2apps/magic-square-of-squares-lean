## The system

For coprime `m > n ≥ 1` and `t = a/b`, four cells of the square are squares identically: `(ma ± nb)²`, `(na ± mb)²`. The other five must be squares:

```
F0 = m²b² + n²a²        F8 = n²b² + m²a²        F4 = s(a² + b²),  s = (m² + n²)/2
L  = F4 − 2mnab         U  = F4 + 2mnab
```

Two facts used below:

- Every 3×3 magic square of rational squares arises this way (derived here, but not new: it is configuration 2 of L. Morgenstern's list, multimagie.com, October 2010, and the formulas are in A. Bremner, Acta Arith. 88 (1999); found by an independent review of `e58a514` through the Kepler71 notes): the four identically square cells are the edge cells, and any two ways of writing a number as a sum of two squares have the form `(ma+nb)² + (na−mb)² = (ma−nb)² + (na+mb)²`. So G1, taken over all `(m : n)`, is the whole problem, cut into curves.
- `(m, n, t)` and `(m+n, m−n, (t+1)/(t−1))` give the same square, scaled by 4, with `F0, F8` and `L, U` exchanged. Pairs therefore come in couples (divide by 2 when `m`, `n` are both odd).

A pair has a **trivial point** when `m² + n²` is a square (`t = ±1`) or `(m² + n²)/2` is a square (`t = 0, ∞`). These are degenerate squares with repeated cells.

**The family as hyperplane sections.** Auel and Singer (arXiv:2609.09351, 2026) study the surface `V ⊂ P⁸` of magic squares of squares in the coordinates `a, …, i` (the roots): a complete intersection of six quadrics, of degree 64, with 256 ordinary double points defined over `ℚ(ζ₈)`.

*Proposition (not in Lean; proved by hand below, not checked by anyone else or by computer algebra).* For `m, n ≠ 0`, the hyperplane section of `V` by `n(b + d) = m(h + f)` is the union of (i) the 16 conics `d = −b`, `f = −h`, `a = ±h`, `i = ±b`, `c = ±e`, `g = ±e`, `b² + h² = 2e²`, and (ii) the curve `C(m : n)` cut out on `V` by the two linear equations `n(b + d) = m(h + f)`, `m(b − d) = n(f − h)`, which is the curve of the ratio `m : n` of this note and has degree 32.

*Proof.* On `V`, `b² + h² = d² + f²`, so `(b + d)(b − d) = (f − h)(f + h)`. On the hyperplane, `b + d = (m/n)(h + f)`, so `(h + f)·(m(b − d) − n(f − h)) = 0`. Hence a point of the section has `h + f = 0`, and then `b + d = 0`, or it lies on `C(m : n)`. If `d = −b` and `f = −h`, the entries satisfy `B = D`, `H = F`; a magic square with these equalities has `A = H`, `I = B` and `C = G = E = (B + H)/2` (twice a corner is the sum of the two edge cells not touching it), which gives (i), with 16 choices of signs. It remains to find the degree of `C(m : n)`. On it, `b = (mu + nv)/2`, `d = (mu − nv)/2`, `f = (nu + mv)/2`, `h = (nu − mv)/2` with `u = (b + d)/m`, `v = (b − d)/n`, and the other five roots satisfy `4a² = n²u² + m²v²`, `4i² = m²u² + n²v²`, `8e² = S(u² + v²)`, `8c² = S(u² + v²) − 4mnuv`, `8g² = S(u² + v²) + 4mnuv`, with `S = m² + n²`. No point of `C(m : n)` has `u = v = 0`, since then all nine coordinates vanish. So `(u : v)` is a morphism from `C(m : n)` to the projective line, and its fibres are the sections of `C(m : n)` by the hyperplanes `βu − αv = 0`, which form a pencil without base point on the curve; hence the degree of the curve is the degree of this morphism. Over a value `(α : β)` at which none of the five binary quadratic forms above vanishes (all but at most ten values), fix `(u, v) = (α, β)`: the four edge coordinates are determined and not all zero, and each of `a, i, e, c, g` has two nonzero values, so the fibre has exactly 2⁵ = 32 points. Hence the degree is 32. Finally `V` has degree 64 and 16·2 + 32 = 64, so each of the 17 curves occurs in the hyperplane section with multiplicity one: the decomposition holds for the section as a divisor, not only as a set. (This uses that every component of the section is one of the conics or lies in `C(m : n)`, shown above, and it does not use or prove that `C(m : n)` is irreducible.) ∎

So G1 is the pencil of hyperplane sections of `V` through these 16 conics. The conics are components of the non-distinct locus of Auel and Singer (their Proposition 3.1 lists 128 conics), and components of degree 32 and genus 49, the genus of `C(m : n)`, occur in their paper as other hyperplane sections. Their paper is about the geometry of `V` (automorphisms, Hodge numbers, the Picard group, projections to del Pezzo, K3 and Enriques surfaces) and has no statement on prime divisors of the entries. Their Lemma 1.2, that a square with distinct entries has no zero entry, follows from `IsMSS.eq_zero_of_exists_eq_zero` here. Bruin, Thomas and Várilly-Alvarado (2022) prove that this surface contains only finitely many curves of genus 0 or 1.

## Result 1: the primes at which the full system can fail modulo `p`

For `p ∤ 2mn(m² − n²)(m² + n²)(m⁸ + 14m⁴n⁴ + n⁸)` the five conditions define a smooth curve of genus 49 over `F_p` (a `(Z/2)⁵` cover of the line branched at ten points; the Kepler71 notes give the same genus and cite Auel–Singer, arXiv:2609.09351). The Weil bound gives at least `p + 1 − 98√p` points, which is positive for `p ≥ 9613`. `classify.c` tests every ratio `(m : n)` for every odd prime below 9700.

The octic factor was missing from this statement before version 0.3.2. Up to squares it is the resultant of `F0` and `GL` (and of `F0`, `GU`; `F8`, `GL`; `F8`, `GU`): at a prime dividing it two branch points collide, the curve modulo `p` is singular, and the bound for a smooth curve does not apply. For such a ratio and `p ∤ 2mn(m⁴ − n⁴)`, `F0·F8·GL·GU` is `(2Smn)²` times the square of a quartic modulo `p`, and no other product of some of the five forms is a constant times a square. Weil's bound for character sums then gives `32N ≥ 2p − 100 − 270√p` for the number `N` of `t` modulo `p` at which all five forms are nonzero squares, which is positive for `p ≥ 18400`; such a `t` lifts to `ℚ_p`. `octic_check.py` checks the identity, and checks directly the 2112 ratios of this kind at the 264 primes between 9700 and 20000 that have one: each has such a `t`. Below 9700 these ratios were among those tested by `classify.c`. So the list of 34 primes is unchanged.

*Derivation of the bound, for a ratio on the octic.* (For the other ratios the ten roots are distinct and the bound for the smooth curve is used.) Let `χ` be the quadratic character modulo `p` and `f₁, …, f₅` the five forms. Modulo `p` their ten roots are six points: `±t₀` (roots of `F0`), `±1/t₀` (roots of `F8`), where `t₀, 1/t₀` are the roots of `GL` and `−t₀, −1/t₀` those of `GU`, and `±i` (roots of `G4`); they are distinct because `p ∤ 2mn(m⁴ − n⁴)`. For `t` not a root, `∏(1 + χ(fᵢ(t)))` is 32 if all five values are squares and 0 otherwise; for the at most 6 roots it is at most 16. So `32N ≥ ∑_I ∑_t χ(∏_{i∈I} fᵢ(t)) − 96`, the outer sum over the 32 subsets `I` of the forms. `I = ∅` contributes `p`. `I = {F0, F8, GL, GU}` contributes at least `p − 4`: the product is a nonzero square away from its four roots, by the identity `S²·F0·F8·GL·GU = (2mn·B)² + 4·(m⁸ + 14m⁴n⁴ + n⁸)·t²·B`, `B = S²(t⁴ + 1) + (2S² − 16m²n²)t²` (Lean: `G1.octic_square`; the four resultants are `G1.resultant_F0_GL` and `G1.resultants_octic`). In each of the other 30 products some root has odd multiplicity (the roots `±i` belong to `G4` only, and each of `±t₀, ±1/t₀` to exactly two of the other four forms, in the pattern above), so the product is not a constant times a square, and Weil's bound `|∑_t χ(g(t))| ≤ (r − 1)√p` for a polynomial `g` with `r` distinct roots that is not a constant times a square gives at least `−9√p` each (a product of at most five quadratics has degree at most 10, so `r ≤ 10`; here the roots are among the six points, so `r ≤ 6` and the constant 9 is not sharp). In total `32N ≥ 2p − 100 − 270√p`. As a test of the main term and of the constants, `octic_check.py` also counts `N` exactly for the 2112 classes between 9700 and 20000: `|32N − 2p|` is at most 0.107 times `100 + 270√p` in every one.

*Which primes are covered by what.* `p < 9700`: every ratio, including `n ≡ 0` and the ratios on the octic, by `classify.c`. `9700 < p < 20000`: ratios off the octic by the Weil bound for the smooth curve (`p ≥ 9613`), ratios on the octic by the direct check in `octic_check.py` (output in `octic_check.txt`). `p > 20000`: the Weil bound, and for ratios on the octic the bound above (`p ≥ 18400`). `octic_check.py` works with `n = 1`: the forms are homogeneous in `(m, n)`, and a ratio on the octic has `n ≢ 0`, since at `n ≡ 0` the octic is `m⁸ ≢ 0`.

**There are exactly 34 primes with an obstructed class, the largest is 607:**

7, 17, 23, 31, 41, 47, 73, 79, 89, 103, 113, 127, 137, 151, 167, 191, 193, 199, 223, 233, 239, 257, 263, 271, 281, 311, 313, 337, 353, 439, 457, 521, 599, 607.

There are 484 obstructed classes in total (`modp_classes.txt`), all at ratios of good reduction (for the octic factor this is checked by `octic_check.py`). All 34 primes are `±1 mod 8`; Result 1b says why. The classes for 17 and 41 agree with the Kepler71 lists.

## Result 1b: the number `m² + n²`

Everything here is about 3×3 squares. "No bound" means no bound on the size of the entries.

**No obstruction modulo a prime `≡ ±3 (mod 8)` (Lean: `G1.exists_point_mod_of_not_isSquare_two`).** In integer form, at `t = 1` the five forms are `m² + n²`, `m² + n²`, `4(m² + n²)`, `4(m − n)²`, `4(m + n)²`, and at `t = 0` they are `m²`, `n²` and three times `2(m² + n²)`. So a class `m : n` can be obstructed modulo `p` only if both `m² + n²` and `2(m² + n²)` are non-residues. If `2` is a non-residue this is impossible. Three remarks on the scope:

- This is a necessary condition for a prime to obstruct. It does not say which primes `±1 (mod 8)` do; that the list of 34 is complete is computed (Result 1).
- It concerns congruences modulo `p` only. Modulo a power of `p` such a prime can obstruct: the condition at primes `≡ 5 (mod 8)` below is an example. Other prime-power obstructions are not studied here.
- The class `n ≡ 0` is never obstructed either: for `(m, n) ≡ (1, 0)` the forms at `(u, v) = (1, 1)` are `1, 4, 1, 4, 4`. So modulo `p` there are `p + 1` classes, and the obstructed ones, `c_p` in number, are among the `p` classes with `n ≢ 0`.

**At 7, 17, 31 and 47 the converse holds (Lean: `G1.isSquare_norm_mod`).** At these four primes every class with `m² + n²` a non-residue is obstructed: 4 of 4 classes, 8 of 8, 16 of 16, 24 of 24. So a solution has `m² + n²` a square modulo 7, 17, 31 and 47 (if `p` divides `m² + n²` this says nothing). This is a reading of the class table, checked by the kernel; I know no reason why it holds at these four primes and fails at the others. The numbers `c_p` are:

7:4, 17:8, 23:8, 31:16, 41:8, 47:24, 73:8, 79:24, 89:28, 103:20, 113:8, 127:8, 137:8, 151:36, 167:24, 191:24, 193:8, 199:8, 223:16, 233:12, 239:8, 257:16, 263:16, 271:24, 281:16, 311:16, 313:16, 337:16, 353:8, 439:16, 457:8, 521:8, 599:8, 607:8 (total 484).

**As conditions on a 3×3 magic square of squares (Lean: `IsMSS.edge_norm`; for the matrix form `IsMagicSquareOfSquares.edge_norm`).** Take a magic square of squares with roots `a b c / d e f / g h i` such that `b ≠ d` and `h + f ≠ 0`. This holds when the nine entries are distinct and the roots are taken positive. Write `|b + d| = g·m`, `|h + f| = g·n` with `g = gcd(b + d, h + f)`. Then

- `m² + n²` is a square modulo 7, 17, 31 and 47;
- every prime `p ≡ 5 (mod 8)` divides `m² + n²` to an even power (this is the statement of the Kepler71 notes, proved in `G1Primes.lean`).

Since `m`, `n` are coprime, `m² + n²` is odd or twice an odd number, and no prime `≡ 3 (mod 4)` divides it. So `m² + n² = 2^ε·k²·c` with `ε ∈ {0, 1}`, `c` squarefree, every prime factor of `c` `≡ 1 (mod 8)`, and `c` a square modulo 7, 31, 47, and modulo 17 unless 17 divides `m² + n²`. `c = 1` means that `m² + n²` is a square or twice a square; these are exactly the pairs with a trivial point. Replacing a root by its negative, or rotating the square, gives another magic square of squares, so the same Lean theorem applies to the ratios `(b − d) : (h + f)`, `(b + d) : (h − f)` and `(d + h) : (f + b)`; these variants are not stated separately.

**The centre (Lean: `IsMSS.norm_dvd_center`, `IsMSS.norm_isSquare_or_prime_dvd_center`).** With `m`, `n` as above there are integers `u`, `v` with `b + d = m·u`, `h + f = n·u`, `b − d = n·v`, `f − h = m·v`, and `8e² = (m² + n²)(u² + v²)`. In Gaussian integers: `2(b + h·i) = (m + n·i)(u − v·i)` and `2(d + f·i) = (m + n·i)(u + v·i)`. Hence every odd prime dividing `m² + n²` divides the centre root `e`, and:

> either `m² + n²` is a square or twice a square, or some prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 8)` divides the centre root and divides `m² + n²` to an odd power.

So a magic square of squares whose centre root has no prime factor `≡ 1 (mod 8)` has both ratios of trivial type (the second ratio is `u : v`, obtained by negating `h`). The theorems need no primitivity and no distinctness beyond `b ≠ d`, `h + f ≠ 0`; these force `e ≠ 0`. The matrix form is `IsMagicSquareOfSquares.centre`.

**The two ratios together (Lean: `IsMSS.edge_params`, `IsMSS.center_factorization`, `IsMSS.edge_sums_identity`, `IsMSS.two_mul_norms_isSquare`, `IsMSS.norms_parity`, `IsMSS.norms_padicVal_parity`, `IsMSS.centre_two_ratios`).** Assume also `f ≠ h`. The second ratio `(b + d) : (f − h)` is `u : v`; write it `m′ : n′` in lowest terms. Then `u = k·m′`, `v = k·n′` with `k ≠ 0`, and

`8e² = (m² + n²)·k²·(m′² + n′²)`.

In terms of the entries this is `((b + d)² + (h + f)²)·((b + d)² + (f − h)²) = 8e²·(b + d)²`, which holds whenever `b² + h² = d² + f² = 2e²`. Consequences, each a Lean theorem:

- `2(m² + n²)(m′² + n′²)` is a square, namely `(4e/k)²`;
- exactly one of the two numbers is even (a sum of two coprime squares is odd or twice an odd number);
- for every odd prime `p`, the exponents of `p` in the two numbers have the same parity;
- either some prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 8)` divides the centre root and divides both numbers to an odd power, or one of the numbers is a square and the other is twice a square.

For the last one: the square with `h` replaced by `−h` is again a magic square of squares, and its first ratio is `m′ : n′`, so the statement on one ratio holds for `m′² + n′²` too.

**Any two lines through the centre (Lean: `TwoLinesSq` and its theorems).** Everything in the list above except the last item uses only `b² + h² = d² + f² = 2e²`. The four lines through the centre are the middle column `(b, h)`, the middle row `(d, f)` and the diagonals `(a, i)`, `(c, g)`, and any two of them satisfy the same pair of equations. `TwoLinesSq b d e f h` is that pair of equations; the parametrisation, the factorisation, the identity in the entries, the square, the parity and the exponent statement are proved for it, and `IsMSS.twoLines`, `IsMSS.twoLines_diagonals`, `IsMSS.twoLines_column_diagonal`, `IsMSS.twoLines_column_antidiagonal`, `IsMSS.twoLines_row_diagonal`, `IsMSS.twoLines_row_antidiagonal` supply the six pairs. For the two diagonals, for example, the ratios are `(a + c) : (i + g)` and `(a + c) : (g − i)`. The last item, with the congruence modulo 8, is specific to the two middle lines: its proof uses a corner.

**The line that a prime divides (Lean: `TwoLinesSq.prime_dvd_line`, `IsMSS.prime_dvd_edge_line`).** An odd prime `p` dividing `m² + n²` to an odd power divides `e` and one of the two lines entirely: `b` and `h`, or `d` and `f`. Proof: `p` divides `u² + v²` (compare exponents in `8e² = (m² + n²)(u² + v²)`), and `4bd = m²u² − n²v² = m²(u² + v²) − (m² + n²)v²`, so `p` divides `b` or `d`; then `b² + h² = 2e²` and `p ∣ e` give the other end.

*Consequence for what is new.* For the middle lines this lemma shows that the prime of the statement on the centre divides the centre and a mid-edge entry. For such a prime of a primitive square, `p ≡ 1 (mod 8)` is known: no prime `≡ 5 (mod 8)` divides a mid-edge entry (Rabern, Theorem 1.4), and Woll's Corollary 4.3 states the congruence for primes dividing the centre and a mid-edge entry. So the congruence in `IsMSS.norm_isSquare_or_prime_dvd_center` and `IsMSS.centre_two_ratios` follows from published results once the lemma is known, and is not new. What these theorems add is the identification of the primes: they are the primes dividing `m² + n²` to an odd power.

**A primitive square (Lean: `IsMSS.primitive_factorization`).** If no prime divides all nine roots, then `k = ±2`:

`b = s(mm′ + nn′)`, `d = s(mm′ − nn′)`, `f = s(nm′ + mn′)`, `h = s(nm′ − mn′)` with `s = ±1`, and `(m² + n²)(m′² + n′²) = 2e²`.

(`k` is even because `8e²` is not twice an odd number; a prime dividing `k/2` divides the four edge roots, hence everything.) This is the composition of sums of two squares, `(mm′ + nn′)² + (nm′ − mn′)² = (mm′ − nn′)² + (nm′ + mn′)²`. The other entries are then `a² = n²m′² + m²n′²`, `i² = m²m′² + n²n′²`, `c² = e² − 2mnm′n′`, `g² = e² + 2mnm′n′` (not in Lean). So a primitive square is the same thing as a pair of ratios for which these five numbers are squares.

**Three kinds of primes (not in Lean).** In a primitive square a prime dividing the centre and two of the four lines divides everything. So each prime divisor of the centre divides nothing else, or exactly one diagonal, or exactly one middle line, and in the last case it is `≡ 1 (mod 8)`. The odd squarefree part `c` of `m² + n²` is a product of primes of the third kind. If no prime divides both the centre and a mid-edge entry, then `c = 1`: both ratios are of the exceptional type below.

In words, the two numbers have the same odd squarefree part `c`, and every prime factor of `c` is `≡ 1 (mod 8)` and divides `e`. What follows in this paragraph is not in Lean. If `c = 1`, write the two numbers as `r²` and `2r′²`; then `2e = ±r·k·r′`, `r ≥ 5`, and if no prime `≡ 1 (mod 8)` divides `e`, every prime factor of `r` and of `r′` is `≡ 5 (mod 8)` (it is `≡ 1 (mod 4)` and divides `e`). The argument does not exclude this case, and I do not know whether it can occur. No congruence on the ratios can exclude it: a ratio with `m² + n²` a square or twice a square is exactly a ratio with a degenerate rational point (`t = ±1`, or `t = 0`), so it is soluble everywhere locally. In the count of pairs of ratios below, 99.8% of the pairs of height at most 6000 that pass all necessary conditions are of this type. Rotating the square does not help: it replaces the two ratios by their partners `(m + n) : (m − n)` and `(m′ + n′) : (m′ − n′)`, and the partner of a ratio whose number is a square has a number that is twice a square, and conversely. That `2(m² + n²)(m′² + n′²)` is a square is the condition that `F4` (the centre) is a square at `t = m′/n′`, written multiplicatively. Searches already impose it, by centre or through the five forms, so it is an arithmetic reading of the centre condition and not a new search method.

**Why 1 modulo 8.** A prime is `≡ 1 (mod 8)` exactly when `−1` and `2` are squares modulo it, that is, when it splits completely in `ℚ(i, √2) = ℚ(ζ₈)`. For a prime `p` dividing `m² + n²` to an odd power, `−1` is a square because `m`, `n` are coprime, and `2` is a square by the statement on primes `≡ 5 (mod 8)` (from the five forms at such a prime). Rabern's proofs use the same two rings, `ℤ[i]` and `ℤ[√2]`.

**Comparison with published statements on the centre.** On 22 November 2026 the following were read with an AI model from the text of the PDFs. In full: Rabern (2002 and 2003 versions), Woll (2018), Hill (2025), Ben Chaffin's page. The statements and the sections that concern the entries: Weisenberg (2023), Bremner (1999, 2001), Auel and Singer (2026, introduction and Section 3), Kominers (introduction), Bruin, Thomas and Várilly-Alvarado (2022) and Várilly-Alvarado's survey (the parts on magic squares). All of these texts were also searched for statements on the primes dividing the centre. What they contain:

- Rabern, Theorem 1.2, and Woll, Theorem 3.1: every prime divisor of the centre of a primitive square is `≡ 1 (mod 4)`.
- Rabern, Theorem 1.3: a prime `≡ 3` or `5 (mod 8)` dividing a non-centre entry divides the centre and the other entry of that line.
- Woll, Corollaries 4.2 and 4.3: a prime dividing the centre and a mid-edge entry is `≡ 1 (mod 8)`. Corollary 5.2: if 2, 5, 13 or 17 divides the centre, it divides another entry.
- Weisenberg, Theorem 2.10, and Woll, Corollary 5.1: a prime dividing the centre and no other entry has three consecutive nonzero quadratic residues. Weisenberg, Theorem 2.4: a prime dividing the three entries of a diagonal to a higher power in a corner than in the other two is `≡ 1 (mod 8)`.
- Bremner's papers construct squares with six and seven square entries and study the surfaces involved; Auel and Singer and Bruin, Thomas and Várilly-Alvarado study the geometry of the surface; Kominers treats squares with centre 0 over number fields. They have no statement on the prime divisors of the centre.

Each of these goes from a prime to its residue class or to the other entries it divides. I did not find in them `m² + n² ∣ 8e²`, the factorisation `8e² = (m² + n²)·k²·(m′² + n′²)`, or a statement that some prime `≡ 1 (mod 8)` divides the centre. With Rabern's Theorem 1.2 the result here reads, for a primitive square: if no prime divisor of the centre root is `≡ 1 (mod 8)`, then all of them are `≡ 5 (mod 8)`, one of the two numbers is a square `r²` with `r ≥ 5`, the other is twice a square, and the prime divisors of `r` divide the centre root.

**Rabern's closing remark.** Rabern ends by saying that one might conjecture that in a solution all prime divisors of all entries are `≡ 1 (mod 8)`, and that it would also be interesting to prove the opposite, that some entry must have a prime divisor `≡ 5` or `7 (mod 8)`. The result here says where a solution stands for its centre: either the centre root has a prime divisor `≡ 1 (mod 8)`, or the solution is of the exceptional type, and then its centre root has a prime divisor `≡ 5 (mod 8)`.

**Density (argued; not in Lean).** Order the coprime pairs `1 ≤ n < m` by `m`. The density of a set of pairs is the limit, if it exists, of its proportion among the pairs with `m ≤ X`. A condition on `m : n` modulo a fixed integer `N` has a density, equal to the proportion of the points of the projective line over `ℤ/N` that satisfy it; for coprime moduli these proportions multiply (Chinese remainder theorem). Sketch of a proof of the first statement (written with an AI model, not checked by anyone else; below, `N` is a product of primes and squares of primes, since the conditions used are conditions on the ratio modulo `p` or `p²`): fix `(a, b)` modulo `N` with `gcd(a, b, N) = 1`. The pairs `(m, n) ≡ (a, b)` in the triangle `1 ≤ n < m ≤ X` number `X²/(2N²) + O(X)`. A common divisor `d` of such a pair is prime to `N`, and the pairs divisible by `d` number `X²/(2d²N²) + O(X/d)`, so by Möbius inversion the coprime ones number `(X²/(2N²))·∏_{p∤N}(1 − p⁻²) + O(X log X)`. This does not depend on `(a, b)`. A point of the projective line over `ℤ/N` has `φ(N)` representatives `(a, b)`, all with `gcd(a, b, N) = 1`, so every point receives the same share of the coprime pairs; there are `N·∏_{p∣N}(1 + 1/p)` points.

- The ratios in none of the 484 classes have density `∏ (1 − c_p/(p + 1)) = 0.0027661`.
- Let `P` be a finite set of primes `≡ 5 (mod 8)`. The ratios for which no `p ∈ P` divides `m² + n²` exactly once form a set defined modulo `∏ p²`, of density `∏ (1 − 2(p − 1)/(p(p + 1)))`. Every ratio that passes the condition at primes `≡ 5 (mod 8)` lies in this set, for every `P`. As `P` grows the product tends to 0, because `∑ 1/p` over primes `≡ 5 (mod 8)` diverges (the primes `≡ a (mod m)`, `gcd(a, m) = 1`, have Dirichlet density `1/φ(m)`: Serre, *A Course in Arithmetic*, chapter VI, §4, Theorem 2; a set `A` of primes of positive Dirichlet density has a divergent sum of reciprocals, because `∑_{p∈A} p⁻ˢ → ∞` as `s → 1⁺` and `∑_{p∈A} 1/p ≥ ∑_{p∈A} p⁻ˢ` for every `s > 1`). So the ratios that pass have density 0. Only finite sets of primes are used, so no sieve with error terms is needed.
- The decay is slow. `scripts/g1_full/density.py` draws 20,000 pairs uniformly from the coprime pairs `1 ≤ n < m ≤ H` (seed 1) and prints the proportion that the second condition does not exclude for `H = 10³, 10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶`: 22.05%, 18.70%, 17.36%, 16.08%.
- For `m ≤ 1000` (same script): 303,204 pairs lie in one of the classes, 237,715 fail the condition at primes `≡ 5 (mod 8)`, 237,153 do both, 303,766 do at least one, 425 do neither. So 562 pairs are excluded by the second condition only. The Lean census uses the second condition only in the form "`p` divides `m² + n²` exactly once", for the 19 primes of `fivePrimes1000` (5, 13, 29, 37, 53, 61, 109, 149, 157, 173, 181, 269, 373, 541, 557, 701, 773, 3461, 12277); the generator picks, for each pair not in a class, the first prime that works, and these 19 are the ones it needed. The 425 exceptions are the same.

This says nothing about whether a magic square of squares exists: a single surviving ratio would be enough.

**Rank 2 and 3 on `E0L`: the argument with four points (`dm4.py`; added after release 0.3.4).** *Status: unreviewed. The same material, with the proofs of the elementary steps written out and a list of what a reader is asked to check, is in [FOUR_POINTS.md](FOUR_POINTS.md).* `C: y² = q(t) = F0·GL` occurs four times in the Jacobian because `F0·GL`, `F0·GU`, `F8·GL`, `F8·GU` define the same curve: `q(−t) = F0·GU`, `t⁴q(1/t) = F8·GL`, `t⁴q(−1/t) = F8·GU`. So a solution `t0` gives four rational points of `C`,

`Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4` with `t`-coordinates `t0, −t0, 1/t0, −1/t0`.

The argument above uses `Q1` and `Q2` and needs rank 1. With all four it works for rank 2 and rank 3. It cannot work for rank 4 or more, because the curve occurs only four times.

*Notation.* `E` is `C` with origin `(t1, y1)`; the involution `y ↦ −y` is `Q ↦ R0 − Q`. Put `a_i = Q_i − R0/2` in `E(ℚ) ⊗ ℚ`, write `N` for the canonical height as a quadratic form and `⟨ , ⟩` for its bilinear form, and `H = h(t0)`. `E` has its full 2-torsion over ℚ; `x` is the coordinate of the model `E_T: Y² = x(x − 2A)(x − 2B)`, `A = m⁴ − 2m³n + 2m²n² + 2mn³ + n⁴`, `B = m⁴ + 2m³n + 2m²n² − 2mn³ + n⁴`, `A·B = m⁸ + 14m⁴n⁴ + n⁸`, which is Connell's model of the quartic with its point `(t1, y1)`, shifted by `6m⁴ + 2n⁴`.

*Three identities* (in the function fields, with `y1² = F0·GL`, `y2² = F0·GU`, `y3² = F8·GL`; `dm4_identities.py` checks the model and the identities with `m`, `n` as indeterminates, by exact computer algebra in `ℚ(m, n)(t)(y1, y2)` and `ℚ(m, n)(t)(y1, y3)`, output in `dm4_identities.txt`; `dm4.py` checks them again for each pair):

- `x(2Q − R0) = 4Π(t)²/(F0·GL)` with `Π = 2mn(n²t² − m²) + (m⁴ − n⁴)t`;
- `x(Q1 + Q2 − R0) = ((y1 − y2)/(2t))²` and `x(Q1 − Q2) = ((y1 + y2)/(2t))²`;
- `x(Q1 + Q3 − R0) = ((y1 − y3)/(t² − 1))²` and `x(Q1 − Q3) = ((y1 + y3)/(t² − 1))²`.

For a magic square of squares with roots `a b c / d e f / g h i` and `t0 = u/v` these read `x(Q1 ∓ Q2 …) = (4a(c ± g)/(uv))²` and `x(Q1 ∓ Q3 …) = (8c(a ± i)/(u² − v²))²`, up to the signs of the roots: the points attached to a square have coordinates that are squares of simple expressions in its entries. This remark is not used below.

A second remark, also not used: on a curve `Y² = x(x² + Ax + B)` a rational point other than the origin and `(0, 0)` has `x` a square exactly when it lies in the image of the 2-isogeny from the curve `Y² = x(x² − 2Ax + A² − 4B)`. So the identities say that `Q1 − Q2`, `Q1 − Q3`, `Q2 − Q4`, `Q3 − Q4` and `2Q − R0` lie in that image: the four points of a solution are in one class modulo the image of this 2-isogeny, which is a subgroup of `E(ℚ)` of 2-power index.

*Heights.* Let `t0 = p/q` in lowest terms with `pq ≠ 0` and `p² ≠ q²`, `M = max(p², q²)`, `Ψ0 = m²q² + n²p²`, `Ψ = S(p² + q²) − 4mnpq`. The identities become `x(2Q − R0) = 2Π(p,q)²/(Ψ0Ψ)`, `x(Q1 ± Q2 …) = 32m²n²Ψ0/(G ± r)` with `G = 2S(p² + q²)` and `r² = G² − 64m²n²p²q²`, and `x(Q1 ± Q3 …) = 2(m² − n²)²Ψ/(G′ ± r′)` with `G′ = S(p² + q²)` and `r′² = G′² − (m² − n²)²(p² − q²)²`. Each denominator `G ± r`, `G′ ± r′` divides a known number (`64m²n²p²q²`, `(m² − n²)²(p² − q²)²`), which bounds the common factor with the numerator; the numerators are positive definite forms. This gives, by elementary estimates written out as Lemmas A, B, C in `FOUR_POINTS.md`:

- `lo4 ≤ h(x(2Q − R0)) − 4H ≤ up4`, with `lo4 = log(n²(m − n)²) − log(2·Res(Π,Ψ0)²·Res(Π,Ψ)²)` and `up4 = log(2(2m³n + m⁴ − n⁴)²)`;
- `−log(2m²) ≤ h(x(Q1 ± Q2 …)) − 2H ≤ log(32m²n²S)`;
- `−log(8(m + n)²) ≤ h(x(Q1 ± Q3 …)) − 2H ≤ log(4(m² − n²)²(m + n)²)`.

*In Lean* (`G1FourPoints.lean`): that the squares in the last two identities are these fractions (`G1.fourPoint_pm_fraction`, `G1.fourPoint_inv_fraction`); that a common divisor of numerator and denominator divides `64m⁴n⁴`, respectively `16(m² − n²)⁴` (`G1.fourPoint_pm_dvd`, `G1.fourPoint_inv_dvd`), which gives the two lower bounds; and that a common divisor of `Π` and `Ψ0` divides `m²n²(m⁸ + 14m⁴n⁴ + n⁸)` and one of `Π` and `Ψ` divides `(m² − n²)²(m⁸ + 14m⁴n⁴ + n⁸)` (`G1.fourPoint_res_psi0_dvd`, `G1.fourPoint_res_psi_dvd`). These are the two resultants in `lo4`; the octic is the one whose prime divisors give the singular fibres of Result 1. Also in Lean: the step from the two resultants to the common factor of `2Π²` and `Ψ0Ψ` (`G1.fourPoint_lemmaC_dvd`), the two lower bounds `max(p², q²) ≤ 2m²·N` and `≤ 8(m + n)²·N` for the reduced numerators `N` (`G1.fourPoint_pm_lower`, `G1.fourPoint_inv_lower`), and the two inequalities of the lattice step below (`G1.fourPoint_rank2_bound`, `G1.fourPoint_rank3_bound`), as statements about real symmetric matrices with a kernel vector. Not in Lean: the three identities on the curve, the upper bounds for the sizes, and everything about heights and the group.

For every rational point `P` of the model, `−U ≤ h(x(P)) − ĥ(P) ≤ BUP`, with the two constants of `height_certify.py` computed for this model (`hbounds.py`). Since `N(a_i) = ĥ(2Q_i − R0)/4` and `4⟨a_1, a_2⟩ = ĥ(Q1 + Q2 − R0) − ĥ(Q1 − Q2)`:

- `N(a_i)` lies in `[H − eL, H + eU]` for the four points, with `eL = (BUP − lo4)/4`, `eU = (up4 + U)/4`;
- `|⟨a_1, a_2⟩|, |⟨a_3, a_4⟩|, |⟨a_1, a_3⟩|, |⟨a_2, a_4⟩| ≤ κ`, with `κ = (BUP + U + w)/4` and `w` the larger of the two widths above.

In words: the four points have almost the same height `h(t0)`, and four of the six pairs are almost orthogonal. The other two pairs, `(Q1, Q4)` and `(Q2, Q3)`, are not controlled this way (their sums and differences live on a curve of genus 3).

*Rank at most 2.* Write `L = H − eL`, `e = eL + eU`. `a_1, a_2, a_4` are linearly dependent, so their Gram determinant vanishes: `N(a_2)·(N(a_1)N(a_4) − ⟨a_1, a_4⟩²) ≤ κ²(N(a_1) + N(a_4) + 2|⟨a_1, a_4⟩|)`. So `a_1` and `a_4` are almost parallel: `z = a_1 − a_4` or `z = a_1 + a_4`, which is a point of `E(ℚ)` (`Q1 − Q4` or `Q1 + Q4 − R0`), has `ĥ(z) ≤ c*(H) = (e²/2 + 4κ²)/L` for `L > 0` (Lean: `G1.fourPoint_rank2_bound`). This decreases to 0 as `H` grows.

*Rank 3.* The Gram matrix of `a_1, a_4, a_2, a_3` is singular. In blocks `[[A, B], [Bᵀ, D]]`, `A` for `(a_1, a_4)` and `D` for `(a_2, a_3)`, all four entries of `B` are among the controlled products. A kernel vector `(u, v)` gives `uᵀAu = −uᵀBv = vᵀDv` and `|uᵀBv| ≤ 2κ|u||v|`, so `A − 2κ` and `D − 2κ` are not both positive definite (Lean: `G1.fourPoint_blocks_not_both_definite`). Hence, for `L > 2κ`, `z = a_1 ∓ a_4` or `z = a_2 ∓ a_3` has `ĥ(z) ≤ c*(H) = 4κ + (e² + 16κe)/(4(L − κ))` (Lean: `G1.fourPoint_rank3_bound`). This decreases to `4κ`.

Both bounds are rational functions of the constants and visibly decreasing in `H`. (The first version of this argument, in the commits of 22 November before this one, used weaker bounds with square roots whose monotonicity the program checked on a grid.)

*The two listings.* Fix `H*`. Either (A) `H < H*`: then `ĥ(2Q1 − R0) ≤ 4(H* + eU)`, and all such points `Q1` are listed, for every torsion point; or (B) `ĥ(z) ≤ c*(H*)`: all such `z` are listed, and for each the equation `t(Q − z)·t(Q) = −1` on `E` is solved (in case (B), `t(Q4) = −1/t(Q1)` and `Q4 = Q1 − z` or `R0 − (Q1 − z)`; for `z = a_2 ∓ a_3` the same equation holds for `Q2`, whose `t` is `−t0`). The equation is a polynomial equation in `t`; its rational roots, the poles of the functions involved and the special positions are all tested. Every candidate `t`, with both signs, goes through the exact five-form test. `t0 ∈ {0, ±1}` and `t0 = ±m/n, ±n/m` are tested directly.

(The method is not new: the Kepler71 notes use Demjanenko–Manin through `z ↦ −z` on rank-1 quotients of their slope family, and quadratic Chabauty on bielliptic curves of genus 2 with quotients `E±` (`FABLE_JOINT_SIEVE`, `FABLE_QC_BIELLIPTIC`, 15 September 2026, github.com/Kepler71/magic-square-of-squares); what may be new here is that `F0·F8·GU` lands on the same two curves as `F0·F8·GL`, their use in the census, and Theorems 1 and 2 of `UNIFORM_RANK.md` (rank at least 1 for every ratio, at least 2 when `m² + n²` is twice a square); nothing else was found to be new.) That curve is palindromic in `t`: with `z = t + 1/t` and `S = m² + n²`,

    F0·F8·GL = t³·g(z),   g(z) = (2Sz − 8mn)·(m²n²z² + (m² − n²)²)

(so `g` is the cubic of `D1` without the factor `z`: `E⁺`, `E⁻` and `D1` are `Y² = λ(z)·g(z)` for `λ = z + 2, z − 2, 2Sz`). Since `z ± 2 = (t ± 1)²/t`, a `t` at which `F0·F8·GL` is a square gives a point with `z = t + 1/t` on `E⁺: Y² = (z + 2)·g(z)` and on `E⁻: Y² = (z − 2)·g(z)`. `F0·F8·GU` is `−t³·g(−z)`, so a `t` at which it is a square gives a point with `z = −(t + 1/t)` on the same two curves: `(−z + 2)·g(−z) = ((t − 1)/t²)²·F0·F8·GU` and `(−z − 2)·g(−z) = ((t + 1)/t²)²·F0·F8·GU`. The factors are rational squares, so these points lie on `E∓` themselves, not on quadratic twists. All of this is in Lean for arbitrary rational `m, n, t` (`G1Sigma.lean`: `sigma_palin`, `sigma_palin_U`, `sigma_plus`, `sigma_minus`, `sigma_plus_U`, `sigma_minus_U`). A `t` at which the four corners are squares therefore gives two points with opposite `z` on `E⁺`, and two on `E⁻`, and the argument of this section applies as soon as one of the two curves has rank exactly 1. The centre is not used.

*The bound* (`scripts/g1_full/dm_sigma.py`; Lean for the algebra). With `q(z) = (z − z0)·g(z)`, `z0 = −2` for `E⁺` and `2` for `E⁻`, and `A = q′(z0)`, write `q = c₁h + c₂h² + c₃h³ + c₄h⁴` in `h = z − z0` (`c₁ = A`). The change `X = A/h`, `y = AY/h²` gives the integral model `y² = X³ + c₂X² + c₁c₃X + c₁²c₄` (`quartic_root_weierstrass`). In it `z ↦ −z` is the involution `X ↦ −AX/(2z0X + A)` (`sigma_involution`); at `X = p/q` in lowest terms its numerator and denominator are at most `(|A| + 4)·max(|p|, |q|)` in absolute value (`moebius_height`), so `|h(X(P′)) − h(X(P))| ≤ log(|A| + 4)`. With `h − ĥ ≤ B` and `ĥ − h ≤ U` (the two sides of the bound, as above), `P = aG + T`, `P′ = a′G + T′`:

    |a² − a′²|·ĥ(G) ≤ B + U + log(|A| + 4),

so when `|a| ≠ |a′|`, `max(|a|, |a′|) ≤ (B + U + log(|A| + 4))/ĥ(G)` (as `|a² − a′²| ≥ |a| + |a′|`). When `|a| = |a′|`, `P′ = ±P + T″` for a torsion point `T″`, and since `X(−Q) = X(Q)`, `X(P + T″) = −AX/(2z0X + A)` with `X = X(P)`. The torsion of every curve used is `{O, (e, 0)}` (checked below). For `T″ = O` this gives `X = 0` or `X = −A/z0`; for `T″ = (e, 0)`, `X(P + T″) = e + f′(e)/(X − e)` (`two_torsion_translate`, with `f` the cubic), and the equation is the quadratic `−AX(X − e) = (e(X − e) + f′(e))·(2z0X + A)`, whose rational roots are candidates. The points at infinity are candidates as well: `P = O` (`z = z0`), `P′ = O` (`X = −A/(2z0)`), and `X = 0` (`z = ∞`, `t = 0` or `∞`). Each candidate `z` gives the `t` with `t + 1/t = z`; each is tested in the four corners and in the five forms of the square.

## Why this sieve does not transfer to the other fibrations

The same surface can be cut into curves in other ways, and I tried the same sieve on three of them. It finds nothing, for a simple reason: every fibre has a rational point, so no congruence can exclude it.

- **Slopes** (the Kepler71 census: cells `1, 1 ± sz, 1 ± rz, 1 ± (s−r)z, 1 ± (s+r)z`). At `z = 0` all nine cells are equal.
- **G2** (the two corner pairs are squares identically; centre and edges must be squares). At `(a, b) = (m + n, m − n)` the five values are `4(m² + n²)²` and `4(m² ± 2mn − n²)²`, each of the last two twice.
- **G3** (one corner pair and one edge pair). `families.py` finds no obstructed class at any prime below 700 and no excluded pair for `m ≤ 200`, the same as for G2.

So G1, where the two edge pairs are the identical squares, is the one fibration of these four in which degenerate points are rare and congruences decide almost every fibre. For the others only global methods (ranks, Chabauty) can work, which is what Kepler71 used for the slopes.

The last two lines are statements about the open problem itself: they exclude every magic square of squares whose edge roots have one of 303,766 ratios, whatever the size of the entries. The signs of the roots are arbitrary, so the same holds for `(b − d) : (h + f)`; in terms of the family, the system is symmetric under exchanging `(m : n)` with `(v : u)`, where `t = u/v` in lowest terms (the letters of `G1Size.lean`), so the parameter `t` of a solution must avoid the excluded ratios as well. The theorems use only `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`.

`scripts/g1_full/gen_lean.py` writes `G1FullCertificates.lean` from `modp_classes.txt`.

**A consequence for size (Lean: `IsMSS.edge_root_gt_500`, in `G1Size.lean`).** By the symmetry just mentioned, a solution in which `(m : n)` and `t` both have height at most 1000 needs both of them among the 425 exceptions. `G1SizeCheck.lean` shows that no two exceptional pairs fit together: for each of the 361,250 combinations one of the five forms is a non-residue modulo one of 18 small primes (3, 5, 11, 13 and others, listed in that file; the 34 sieve primes are all ±1 mod 8 and are poor at this job). So in a magic square of squares with `b² ≠ d²`, `h² ≠ f²` and `b² ≠ f²`, one of the edge roots `b, d, f, h` is larger than 500 in absolute value. Published computer searches exclude far larger squares; this is only the bound that follows from the theorems here.
